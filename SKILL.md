# The Four Causes

Aristotle held that a thing is understood when four questions about it are answered: what it is,
what it is made of, what brings it about, and what it is for. Answering all four in order turns a
loose impression into a description that can be checked, argued with, and acted on.

This skill is that discipline applied to an answer.

## When to use it

Use it when the question has depth to lose:

- Definitions and explanations — "what is X", "how does X work"
- Comparisons — "what is the difference between X and Y", "which should I pick"
- Causal and feasibility questions — "why did X happen", "is X worth it", "should we do X"
- Planning, analysis, and structuring a body of text
- Building a thing or a representation of a thing — a document, a deck, a page, a brief

Apply it whether or not the user names the method. The output should read as a good answer, not
as a philosophy exercise.

## When to skip it

Skip it — answer plainly instead — when there is nothing for it to hold:

- A short factual question with a short factual answer
- Routine code, or straightforward technical execution
- Small talk
- Any case where the frame would add length without adding understanding

Skipping is a real option, not a formality. Four headings over a one-line answer is a worse answer.

## The four causes

### 1. Formal — what it is

The structure of the thing: its parts and how they are arranged.

**Components**, sorted by how reliably they appear:

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Essential** | Remove it and the thing stops being itself |
| **Common** | Present in most instances, but not required |
| **Rare** | Appears in some instances; marks a variant |
| **Background** | Context the thing sits in rather than a part of it |

**Arrangement**, four questions about how those components sit together:

- **Order** — what follows what, in sequence or hierarchy
- **Position** — where each part sits relative to the others
- **Geometry** — the shape of the whole, and the shape of its parts
- **Dimensions** — extent, scale, proportion

### 2. Material — what it is made of

The substance, with quantities. Name the stuff, then give numbers: mass, volume, temperature,
duration, count, concentration — whatever the domain measures in. An unquantified material cause
is usually an incomplete one.

For abstract subjects the material is whatever the thing is built out of: data, capital, labour,
attention, code, time.

### 3. Efficient — what brings it about

The forces acting on the thing, in two columns:

- **Enables** — what causes, drives, accelerates, or sustains it
- **Prevents** — what blocks, slows, degrades, or ends it

Name the mechanism, not just the factor. "Heat" is a factor; "heat drives off the solvent, so the
film sets" is a mechanism.

### 4. Final — what it is for

The purpose, judged in two columns:

- **Benefits** — what it achieves, and for whom
- **Drawbacks** — what it costs, risks, or forecloses

## Rules

**An empty slot stays empty — or gets a reason.** If a cause does not apply to the subject, leave
it blank or say in one line why it does not apply. Never pad a slot to fill the form. A blank slot
is information; an invented one is noise.

**Depth follows the question.** The four headings are a skeleton, not a quota. A simple subject
gets a short pass through all four; a complex one gets a long pass. Neither gets filler.

**End with a synthesis.** After the four causes, close with a short passage that puts them back
together: what the four answers add up to, what tension they expose, what follows from them. This
is required. An analysis that stops at the fourth heading has taken the thing apart without
showing what it is.

## Output shape

Default to four headings plus a synthesis. Adapt the presentation to the subject — prose for a
concept, a table for a comparison, a list for components — but keep the order and keep the
synthesis last.

For a comparison, run the causes across both subjects rather than answering twice: the differences
show up inside each cause, which is where they are legible.
